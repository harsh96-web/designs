The problem

iOS 26 wrapped the whole interface in Liquid Glass — a translucent material that's beautiful, but lets the background show through the text sitting on top of it.

Over a plain wallpaper that's fine. Over a busy or bright photo, in sunlight, or for anyone with reduced vision, the text drops below the contrast needed to read it — WCAG asks for 4.5:1. It's a legibility cost on the one interface everyone uses, in every condition.

Apple agrees, and has kept adding controls — Tinted, Reduce Transparency, even a Reduce Bright Effects toggle. But every one of them is global, manual, and static: you set a single level for the whole system, by hand, and it never changes. Set it heavy enough for your busiest wallpaper and it's needless everywhere else; set it light and it still fails on the busy ones. The glass fails only in certain contexts — so the real fix has to be per-context and automatic, which no fixed setting can be.

Contrast across the full brightness range

Each curve is the measured WCAG contrast for one approach as the background goes from dark to bright, at the current surface and conditions. The dashed line is the active threshold; the vertical marker is where you are now.

iOS 26 Clear (default) iOS 26.1 Tinted (Apple's global fix) Adaptive (our proposal) WCAG threshold

How to read it: we sample the wallpaper behind each glass panel, composite the panel's dark tint over it at its current opacity, add any sunlight veiling glare, then compute the real WCAG ratio against the white text. The argument the chart makes: Clear dips below the line across a wide bright region; Tinted is a single fixed step, so it sits wastefully high on the easy (dark) end yet can still fall short at the bright extreme; only Adaptive tracks just above the line everywhere — glassy where it can be, opaque only where it must be. Under sunlight glare, raising opacity alone hits a ceiling, so the adaptive material climbs a ladder (opacity → text scrim → glare boost) and keeps clearing the line exactly where the default and Apple's fixed Tinted both collapse. The low-vision lens raises the bar to AAA 7:1, where the gap between a fixed setting and an adaptive one becomes impossible to miss. Worth noting: WCAG is explicit that text over translucent and gradient surfaces must still meet 4.5:1 — but it offers no method to measure it and advises testing wherever contrast is lowest, which is exactly what this tool does.
